Harris corner detector

This page is based on the Computer Vision course at FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg. A corner is a place where the brightness changes strongly in two different directions. An edge changes in only one direction, and a flat region changes in neither. This page computes the Harris response on one portrait and marks the corners you ask for.

Try this

  • Look at the red circles. They are the corners for the default settings.
  • Raise the response threshold, then press Run. Fewer, stronger corners remain.
  • Raise the integration sigma. The corners move apart and some weak ones disappear.
  • Open How it works for the structure tensor, and Intermediate pictures for IxIy, which is signed.
How it works (technical details)

I is grayscale brightness, with the same weights as on the edge-detection page: 0.299 red + 0.587 green + 0.114 blue. x increases to the right and y increases downward. Ix is positive when the image grows brighter toward the right. Iy is positive when it grows brighter downward. These derivatives are the 3 × 3 Sobel correlation used on that page, with the edge pixel repeated at the border.

An optional Gaussian of standard deviation σd and odd width kd smooths I before those derivatives. That is the derivative scale. If kd is 1, there is no pre-smoothing. The products Ix2, Iy2 and IxIy are then smoothed by a second Gaussian, standard deviation σi and width ki. That is the integration scale. The two scales are not the same control. Both Gaussians are normalised to sum to one and use mirrored borders (reflect-101). They are not rounded to integers.

A, B and C are the integrated products gi ∗ Ix2, gi ∗ Iy2 and gi ∗ IxIy. C is signed. R equals det M − α (trace M)2. The eigenvalues λ1 and λ2 of M say which kind of place this is: both small means flat, one large and one small means an edge, and both large means a corner. R is large and positive when both eigenvalues are large. It is negative on a clean edge and near zero on a flat region. It is not a probability.

A pixel is kept only when R is strictly greater than t times the largest positive response in this image, and it is a spatial maximum in the chosen odd neighbourhood. The threshold is therefore relative to this picture. Changing the derivative scale changes the size of R, so the same fraction is not an absolute unit. Neighbours that would lie outside the image are not compared. If several pixels tie for the maximum, the one with the smallest row-major index is kept. Display maps are scaled to their own extreme for viewing. That scaling is not the test above.

Limitations. The derivatives are a fixed 3 × 3 Sobel, not a derivative of a Gaussian at scale σd. α is a set constant, usually near 0.04 to 0.06; it is not estimated from the image. There is no sub-pixel refinement. Discrete sampling means the detector is only approximately unchanged by rotation. Reference: Harris, C. and Stephens, M. (1988). A combined corner and edge detector. Proceedings of the Alvey Vision Conference, pp. 147–151.

Settings

Choose the settings first. The numbers update as you move a control. The pictures change only when you press Run.

1.0

Smooths the image before Sobel. Ignored when the derivative window is off.

3 × 3

Odd width of that pre-smoothing. 1 means no pre-smoothing.

1.5

Smooths Ix², Iy² and IxIy. This is not the derivative scale.

5 × 5

Odd width of the integration Gaussian. A short window cuts the tails.

0.04

Larger α makes edges more negative and keeps fewer corners.

0.08

Keep R greater than t times the largest positive R in this image.

5 × 5

A corner must be the maximum of R inside this odd window.

Intermediate pictures

Signed pictures use mid-gray for zero. Non-negative pictures use black for zero and white for their own maximum. These scales are for viewing. IxIy and C are signed and can be dark where the product is negative.
