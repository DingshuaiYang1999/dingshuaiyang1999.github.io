Pinhole Camera Model: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Parameters

This page is based on the Computer Vision course at FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg. A pinhole camera turns a 3D point into a pixel. The focal length and the sensor fix two of the entries of K. Five numbers inside K stretch, shear and shift the picture, and six more aim the camera. Drag the view on the left. The sliders change the photograph on the right, and they are not moved for you.

Try this

  • Lower f. The field of view widens and the tree gets smaller. fx and fy move with it.
  • Change only Sw. The horizontal angle and fx change. fy stays, because the pixels are no longer square.
  • Set s away from zero. Vertical lines of the tree lean: that is shear.
  • Open the aperture (a smaller f-number). The tree stays sharp. The gifts in front and the house behind go soft. Move the focus distance onto the house to sharpen the far objects.
How it works (technical details)

Principle

A scene point is a 4-vector. The camera writes it in camera axes, multiplies by K, and divides by the third entry. Points with \(z_{\mathrm{cam}} \le 0\) are behind the camera and are not drawn. Lens distortion is ignored.

Formulas

\[ w\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = K\begin{bmatrix}R & t\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y \\ z \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \]
\[ K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & u_0 \\ 0 & f_y & v_0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Lens and field of view

Sw and Sh are the sensor width and height, and f is the focal length. All three are in millimetres. The photograph is W × H pixels, here 720 × 480, the same 3:2 shape as the reset sensor 36 mm × 24 mm. At that reset, px = py and fx = fy.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{AFOV}_h &= 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{S_w}{2f}\right), \\ \operatorname{AFOV}_v &= 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{S_h}{2f}\right). \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} p_x &= \frac{S_w}{W}, & p_y &= \frac{S_h}{H}, \\ f_x &= \frac{fW}{S_w}, & f_y &= \frac{fH}{S_h}. \end{aligned} \]

So \(\frac{S_w}{2f}=\frac{W}{2f_x}\) and \(\frac{S_h}{2f}=\frac{H}{2f_y}\). The centred pixel formula is the same ratio. It assumes \(s=0\) and a principal point at the centre. If the principal point is not centred, the horizontal angle about the optical axis is \(\arctan\!\left(\frac{u_0}{f_x}\right)+\arctan\!\left(\frac{W-u_0}{f_x}\right)\), and the vertical angle is \(\arctan\!\left(\frac{v_0}{f_y}\right)+\arctan\!\left(\frac{H-v_0}{f_y}\right)\). These angles ignore skew.

W and H are adjustable. Changing W sets \(f_x=\frac{fW}{S_w}\) and puts \(u_0=W/2\). Changing H sets \(f_y=\frac{fH}{S_h}\) and puts \(v_0=H/2\). \(\operatorname{AFOV}_h\) and \(\operatorname{AFOV}_v\) stay constant because they depend only on \(S_w\), \(S_h\) and \(f\). If \(W/H\ne S_w/S_h\), then \(p_x\ne p_y\), and therefore \(f_x\ne f_y\). Changing either focal-length slider changes the shared \(f\), so the other focal length follows. The full-frame equivalent \(36f/S_w\) is only a label. In the world view, the lens-to-sensor gap is drawn 28 times life size so the millimetres can be seen. The angle is the true field of view.

Rotation around the coordinate axes, counter-clockwise

Positive angles are counter-clockwise about the axis. α, β and γ are sometimes called pitch, yaw and roll. The sliders use only Rx(α), Ry(β) and Rz(γ).

\[ \begin{aligned} R_x(\alpha) &= \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\0&\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{bmatrix}, \\[5pt] R_y(\beta) &= \begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta&0&\sin\beta\\0&1&0\\-\sin\beta&0&\cos\beta\end{bmatrix}, \\[5pt] R_z(\gamma) &= \begin{bmatrix}\cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma&0\\\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

The widget below uses \(R=R_z(\gamma)R_x(\alpha)R_y(\beta)\) and \(\mathbf p'=R\mathbf p+\mathbf t\). The photograph uses that same product on the right by \(R_0=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1)\), so \(X_{\mathrm{cam}}=RR_0X+\mathbf t\). That sends world y up to camera y down and world z toward the viewer to camera z forward. The matrices match the counter-clockwise rotations in Silvio Savarese’s lecture, as used at FAU.

Aperture, beyond the pinhole model

The f-number and the focus distance are not among the eleven parameters of K[R | t]. They do not move the centre of a point’s image. Blur uses the thin-lens circle of confusion, with \(A=f/N\) the aperture diameter, \(d=z_{\mathrm{cam}}\) and \(d_f\) the focus distance, with \(f,d,d_f\) in the same unit:

\[ c=\frac{Af\left|d-d_f\right|}{d\left(d_f-f\right)} \]

The photograph turns c into pixels with the horizontal pitch, \(c/p_x\), and blurs each depth band with a Gaussian whose radius is about half that diameter, capped at 16 px. Brightness is not scaled by aperture area: a factor \(A^2\) would make the picture black as \(A\to0\), so the closed aperture would not match the pinhole colours. With that choice, \(A\to0\) reproduces the \(K[R\mid t]\) image exactly. Approximations: one depth per face, a few depth bands, a Gaussian rather than a uniform disc, no distortion, and \(d=z_{\mathrm{cam}}\).

At the current aperture and focus, the circle of confusion is listed here for the tree, the near gifts, and the house.

Reading the two views

Drag the world view to orbit it. The camera on the left is a real-scale body on a tripod. Three legs stay on the ground, the centre column lengthens or shortens with the camera height, and the head follows R. A small inset shows the head up close. The tripod is not in the photograph. The rectangle in front of the lens is an empty image plane: it shows where the sensor plane sits and how large the field is, and it carries no picture. The picture is only the photograph on the right, rendered from the same K and [R | t], including skew. Red, green and blue are the world axes. The centre C = −RT t stays at least 0.3 m above the ground and outside the tree, house, figures, gifts, fence and lamp; a slider that would cross that boundary stops there.

The photograph is filled and lit in pixel coordinates (u, v). Grey lines, drawn when the pose or K differs from the reset, are the reset K and the reset [R | t]. Blur follows the circle of confusion and does not move a point’s centre. The red cross is the principal point. The near gifts sit to the side of the tree so the trunk stays visible while they are still close enough to blur at f/1.8.

Snowflakes are points in the world, a centimetre or two across, drifting downward. Both views draw those same points. The photograph projects each flake with K[R | t] and widens it by its circle of confusion, so near flakes blur and the flakes at the focus distance stay small. The sliders never move on their own. If the browser asks for reduced motion, the flakes stay where they are.

Symbols and degrees of freedom

fx, fy, s, u0 and v0 are the five intrinsic degrees of freedom. Rx(α), Ry(β), Rz(γ) and the three entries of t are the six extrinsic degrees of freedom. Dragging the left view changes none of them. The count of eleven, and the split into five plus six, follows the lecture “Degrees of freedom” by James Hays, as used in the FAU course. The worked example is the tip of the main tree.
