Edge detection: Sobel, Canny and the Laplacian

This page is based on the Computer Vision course at FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg. An edge is a place where brightness changes quickly. This page is a revision aid for three detectors on one portrait. Sobel estimates the first derivative. The Laplacian estimates the second derivative and can mark where that response changes sign. Canny thins a first-derivative response and keeps the strong, connected edges. The pictures do not share one brightness scale.

Try this

  • Raise Blur, then press Play demo. Noise fades, and so does fine detail. The Laplacian zero-crossings lose speckle first, because a second derivative amplifies noise.
  • Change the two Canny thresholds and press Play demo. Both Canny pictures use those thresholds. Sobel and the Laplacian stay put.
  • Change Laplacian marker T and press Play demo. The absolute-threshold picture and the zero-crossing picture both change, and they do not become the same picture.
  • Compare Sobel x with Sobel y, then the three pictures under “Three detectors”. Those three outputs are different kinds of measurement.
How it works (technical details)

The notes follow the calculation. Grayscale and the Gaussian blur are shared. Sobel and Canny are a first-derivative path. The Laplacian is a separate second-derivative path on that same blurred image. It is not a step inside Canny. A drawing is labelled as a drawing. It is not the array stored in the program.

Symbols and axes

I is the grayscale image. x increases to the right. In the photograph, and in every discrete kernel below, y increases downward: the first row of a matrix multiplies the pixels above the centre, and the first column multiplies the pixels to its left. In the four surface drawings, y increases upward, as on a graph. Gx and Gy are the Sobel responses. Where a formula writes Ix and Iy, those names mean these same two responses. σ is the Gaussian standard deviation. T is the Laplacian marker.

Grayscale

The later filters need one brightness per pixel. The weights are those used by cv2.cvtColor.

Gaussian blur

A derivative strengthens high frequencies, and a second derivative strengthens them more. The blur is there to hold that noise down before either derivative. The drawing is the continuous normalised Gaussian at σ = 1. Height is the value of the function. The colour only repeats that the whole bell is positive.

The program does not evaluate that formula on the plane. It samples a one-dimensional Gaussian on an odd window of length k, divides by the sum of those samples, and applies the window to rows and then to columns. The border mode is mirror-without-repeating-the-edge-pixel (BORDER_REFLECT_101). If k is short for the chosen σ, the tails are cut off and the blur is not the continuous Gaussian of that σ. If k is 1, the blur is skipped. Sobel, Canny and the Laplacian then all use this one blurred image.

Sobel kernels

Sobel estimates the direction in which brightness increases. Each matrix below is the correlation kernel used by the code: the entry multiplies the pixel in that same place, with no flip. Convolution with a flipped copy is a different convention and is not what this page runs. The kernels are symmetric in the sense needed for a derivative, but they are not symmetric under a 180° turn, so correlation and convolution would not give the same picture.

A positive Gx means the image grows brighter from left to right, so vertical edges are the ones this kernel marks. A positive Gy means it grows brighter from top to bottom, so horizontal edges are marked. Outside the frame, the missing neighbour is a copy of the edge pixel. The Sobel panels show the absolute value unless “Show Sobel as signed” is on, in which case zero is mid-gray. Those two panels share one scale.

Continuous derivatives, and what Sobel actually is

The next two drawings are partial derivatives of the same Gaussian, again at σ = 1. Each uses its own vertical range so the lobes are visible, and the tick numbers are the true values. They are not the 3 × 3 matrices above.

Positive lobe Negative lobe Zero

A derivative of a Gaussian is one smooth filter. This page does not apply that filter. It blurs with the sampled window described above, then correlates with the Sobel integers. Those integers are a small discrete approximation to a smoothed first derivative. They are not samples of these surfaces, and the slider σ is not fixed at 1.

The sign still has a definite relationship, and it is easy to mix up. As a function, ∂G/∂x is positive for negative x and negative for positive x. Convolution with that function would flip the kernel left to right. The Sobel matrix is already the correlation kernel that the program multiplies onto the neighbourhood: its right-hand column is positive, so dark-on-the-left and bright-on-the-right gives a positive Gx. The drawing and the matrix agree only after that flip is taken into account. They are not two pictures of one array. Neither construction is claimed here to be the better edge estimator for every image.

Three norms of the gradient

The length of (Ix, Iy) can be written with three norms. They are not the same function. For every real pair, the max norm is at most the Euclidean norm, and the Euclidean norm is at most the L1 norm. The three numbers agree in special cases, for example when one component is zero. A threshold chosen for one norm does not have the same meaning for the others.

What the page actually calculates is narrower than this list. The gradient-magnitude panel, and the Sobel picture in the three-detector comparison, always show the Euclidean norm divided by its own maximum. Ticking “L2 gradient” does not change those pictures. Canny’s internal strength is the L1 norm when the box is clear, which is also OpenCV’s default. When the box is ticked, Canny stores Gx² + Gy² and squares both thresholds before the comparison, so a pixel passes that test exactly when its Euclidean length exceeds the slider. The max norm is defined above and is not computed anywhere in the demo. The direction used later is θ = atan2(Gy, Gx). The program does not call atan2. It uses an equivalent comparison of the two components.

Canny

Canny’s purpose is to turn a thick strength image into a binary map of thin, connected edges. cv2.Canny does not blur by itself. This page blurs first, then runs the same sequence of steps. The browser does not call OpenCV. A pixel-by-pixel check of a NumPy version against cv2.Canny lives in the Python repository, not in this page. Reference code: edge detection in Python (GitHub, opens in a new tab).

  • Non-maximum suppression. The gradient direction is quantised to four angles: horizontal, vertical, and the two diagonals. On a horizontal or vertical direction a pixel is kept when its strength is strictly greater than one neighbour and at least as large as the other. On a diagonal both comparisons are strict. A tie can therefore leave two pixels, and junctions are not a single ideal line. The edges are thin. They are not one pixel wide in every case.
  • Double threshold. Above the high slider the pixel is strong. Between the low and high sliders it is weak. Otherwise it is discarded. Under the L2 option those comparisons use the squared strength and the squared slider values.
  • Hysteresis. A weak pixel is kept only when an 8-connected chain of weak or strong pixels reaches a strong pixel. Isolated faint responses drop out. A faint continuation of a strong contour can remain.

The Euclidean magnitude picture is still several pixels thick, because nothing has thinned it. One global threshold on that picture either keeps noise or breaks contours. That is the reason for the two Canny thresholds, not a claim that the magnitude itself is a finished edge map. The magnitude panel is scaled to its own brightest pixel for display. That display scale is not the scale used by the thresholds.

A second Canny uses the same grayscale portrait, the same σ, the same window length, the same norm and the same two thresholds, but its gradients are samples of the derivatives of a Gaussian rather than a blur followed by Sobel. The analytic ∂G/∂x is positive on the left of the origin. The kernel that is applied is the correlation form of that derivative: the weight on the right is positive, and the weight below the centre is positive, so the sign matches Sobel. The derivative taps are antisymmetric and therefore sum to zero. A constant image gives a zero response. A window of length 1 has no neighbours, so that response is zero. Mirrored borders (reflect-101) are used. Sobel’s 3 × 3 stage instead repeats the edge pixel, and the blur that precedes it rounds to integers. Those are different discretisations.

On a unit ramp, brightness increasing by 1 per pixel, interior Sobel returns 8. The sampled derivative-of-Gaussian kernels are multiplied by one constant, computed from the kernels alone, so that their unit-ramp response is also 8. The constant does not depend on the picture, and neither gradient is divided by its own maximum before the thresholds. A short window omits the Gaussian tails, so the constant differs from the infinite-support value; after scaling, the ramp gain of the kernel that is actually used is still 8. Shared thresholds are therefore comparable as responses to a unit slope. They are not a claim that the two edge maps are equal.

Laplacian

A large first derivative is one definition of an edge. Another is a zero of the second derivative: the response changes sign across the edge. The continuous statement is

The program replaces each second derivative by a three-point difference. Adding those two kernels, entry by entry, gives the four-neighbour kernel that the demo applies. Every kernel in this group has a negative centre and positive neighbours, and the weights of each kernel sum to zero, so a constant patch gives a zero response. With the negative centre, a bright peak gives a negative response and a dark valley gives a positive response. Because these kernels are unchanged by a 180° turn, correlation and convolution are the same operation for them. The border again repeats the edge pixel.

The weighted kernel is not the unweighted eight-neighbour kernel, whose every neighbour is 1 and whose centre is −8. That unweighted kernel is not used either. There is no control that switches kernels. OpenCV’s cv2.Laplacian with ksize = 1 uses the same four-neighbour array as this demo. It does not build the zero-crossing picture.

The wireframe below is the Laplacian of the continuous Gaussian at σ = 1. The centre is a negative bowl. The response is zero on x² + y² = 2. Outside that circle the ring is positive, and its true height is smaller than the depth of the bowl. This drawing uses its own vertical range; the tick numbers are the true values.

The demo does not sample this surface and does not convolve the portrait with it. It takes the blurred image already computed from the sliders — whatever σ and window are selected, with the default σ equal to 1.4 — and correlates that image with the four-neighbour kernel. The shared fact is the sign convention: a negative centre and a positive surround. The numbers are not the same, and the page does not claim that they are.

Four pictures are shown, and the names are meant literally.

  • Signed response. Zero is mid-gray. Dark is negative (the sign of a bright peak under this kernel). Bright is positive. This is not an edge map.
  • Absolute response. Bright means a large second derivative of either sign, scaled to this panel’s own maximum. Centres of regions can be bright here. This is not an edge map and not a zero-crossing.
  • Absolute threshold. White where the absolute response is at least T. At T = 0 the panel is entirely white. This is a threshold of the magnitude. It is not a zero-crossing picture.
  • Zero-crossings. White only where two 4-connected samples are not the same strict sign and their difference is at least T. The mark is placed on the sample closer to zero. These are the Laplacian edges used in the three-detector comparison.

Raising the blur removes weak zero-crossings that come from noise, and then it removes real fine edges as well. That is the practical form, on this page, of a single-scale Laplacian of a Gaussian. It is not the multi-scale procedure of Marr and Hildreth, and it is not an analytic LoG kernel.

The three comparison pictures

All three start from the same portrait, the same grayscale conversion, and the same blurred image. After that they diverge. The Sobel comparison picture is the Euclidean magnitude, scaled to its own brightest pixel: a thick, continuous strength image. The Canny picture is binary: white means kept by hysteresis, black means discarded. The Laplacian picture is also binary, but the test is a zero-crossing whose jump is at least T, not a Canny threshold and not a threshold of the absolute Laplacian. Equal brightness in two of these pictures does not mean an equal physical quantity. Press Play demo after editing. That click uses the selected values and does not move the controls. Blur is shared by all three pictures. The Canny thresholds affect only Canny. T affects only the absolute-threshold picture and the zero-crossings.

Three detectors, one picture

Same portrait and the same blurred grayscale. Sobel here is the Euclidean magnitude, scaled to its own brightest pixel for display only. Canny is the blur-then-Sobel detector. Two Canny gradients, one pair of thresholds

Both use the selected σ, the selected window length, the selected norm and the two Canny thresholds. The left path blurs, rounds to integers, then applies Sobel. The right path samples the derivatives of a Gaussian on the grayscale image and scales those kernels so that a unit brightness ramp has gain 8, the same gain as Sobel. That scale is fixed by the kernels. It is not taken from this portrait. The maps need not agree.

Reading the panels

In the Sobel panels, bright means a strong first derivative in that direction, or in every direction for the magnitude. In the Canny picture and the Laplacian zero-crossing picture, white means a kept edge and black means the rest; the tests that produce those whites are different. In the signed Laplacian, mid-gray is zero. In the absolute Laplacian, bright means strong curvature of either sign, which includes the centres of regions and is not by itself an edge.

Privacy

No picture is uploaded and nothing is sent to any server: the page loads the portrait that is already part of this website and all calculations run in your browser. The Laplacian uses that same portrait. There is no upload, no camera and no second image.
